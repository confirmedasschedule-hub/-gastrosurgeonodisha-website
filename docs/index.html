About Me

Trained across India.

Practising at home in Odisha.

Prof. Premananda Patnaik grew up in a modest family and studied in government schools. In a single year, without coaching, he cleared four medical entrance exams and topped one of them. That began a long road: medicine at Madras Medical College, surgery at PGIMER Chandigarh, complex GI and cancer surgery at GB Pant Hospital, and a first rank into the superspeciality course at AIIMS New Delhi, where he completed his MCh in GI surgery and liver transplantation.

He came back to Odisha to do this work here. At IMS & SUM Hospital he started the state's first MCh training programme in surgical gastroenterology and performed Odisha's first liver transplant. Today he leads surgical gastroenterology at Hi-Tech Medical College & Hospital and sees patients at Divine Superspeciality Clinic in Jagamara.

    Recent cases at Hi-Tech Medical College

    Case / Spleen

    A 7 kg spleen, removed safely

    A 32-year-old man came after visiting many hospitals with unbearable abdominal pain and repeated vomiting of blood. Tests showed a spleen of about 7 kg, enlarged because the blood supply to the liver was blocked, and it was causing the pain and the bleeding. The operation was challenging and risky. It was completed safely, without complications, and he went home well.

    Case / Bowel

    A swallowed aloe vera stick, removed from the bowel

    A 62-year-old man swallowed raw aloe vera as an ayurvedic remedy. A piece lodged in the last part of his intestine, causing heavy bleeding from the back passage and injuring the bladder and rectum. In a complex operation the damaged bowel and the aloe vera stick were removed and the bladder was repaired. His life was saved and he went home.

    Visits & surgery

    01What does a GI surgeon treat?

    A GI (gastrointestinal) surgeon operates on the digestive system: the food pipe, stomach, liver, gall bladder and bile ducts, pancreas, small intestine, colon and rectum, and the abdominal wall (hernia). Prof. Patnaik treats both cancers and non-cancer problems of these organs, such as gall bladder stones, pancreatitis, liver abscess and cysts, piles, fissure and hernia.

    02How do I book an appointment?

    Call or WhatsApp the clinic on +91 98276 76286. The clinic gives you a time for a consultation at Divine Superspeciality Clinic, Jagamara, Bhubaneswar. Surgery, when needed, is done at Hi-Tech Medical College & Hospital, Bhubaneswar.

    03What should I bring to my first visit?

    Bring all previous reports, blood tests, scan reports and films or CDs (ultrasound, CT, MRI, endoscopy), biopsy reports, discharge summaries, and a list of the medicines you take. Tell the doctor if you take blood thinners or have diabetes, heart, kidney or lung disease.

    04Can I send my reports before visiting?

    Yes. Use Ask your query on this page to send your question on WhatsApp, then attach photos of your reports in the same chat. For a full opinion, the doctor still needs to examine you in person.

    05What is laparoscopic (keyhole) surgery?

    The operation is done through a few small cuts with a camera and fine instruments instead of one large cut. For most patients this means less pain, smaller scars, a shorter hospital stay and a quicker return to normal life. Whether it suits you depends on your condition and health, and is decided after examination and tests.

    06Do gall bladder stones always need an operation?

    Stones that cause pain, infection, jaundice or pancreatitis are usually treated by removing the gall bladder laparoscopically, because medicines do not reliably dissolve stones and attacks tend to come back. Stones found by chance that cause no symptoms do not always need surgery. The doctor advises after looking at your scan and symptoms.

    07Can I live normally without a gall bladder?

    Yes. The liver keeps making bile, which then flows straight into the intestine. Most people go back to a normal diet. A few notice looser stools or discomfort after very oily food for some weeks, which usually settles.

    08How long is the recovery after surgery?

    It depends on the operation. After common keyhole operations such as gall bladder removal or hernia repair, many patients go home within one to two days and return to light work in about one to two weeks. Major cancer, liver or pancreas operations need a longer stay and recovery. You are given a clear plan for diet, activity and follow-up before discharge.

    09Does Prof. Patnaik treat cancer?

    Yes. He operates on cancers of the food pipe, stomach, liver, gall bladder, bile duct, pancreas, colon and rectum, using open, laparoscopic or robotic surgery as suits each case. Surgery is planned together with chemotherapy, radiation and follow-up where needed.

    10Which symptoms need urgent care?

    Go to the nearest hospital or call at once for severe or worsening belly pain, vomiting blood, black or bloody stools, yellow eyes with fever and shivering, a swollen belly with no passing of stool or gas, or a painful lump in the groin that will not go back. Do not wait for a message reply in these situations.

    Pancreatitis & cancer awareness

    11What is pancreatitis?

    Pancreatitis is inflammation of the pancreas, the gland behind the stomach that makes digestive juices and insulin. Acute pancreatitis comes on suddenly and most people recover fully. Chronic pancreatitis is long-lasting damage that can cause repeated pain, poor digestion and diabetes.

    12What causes pancreatitis?

    The two most common causes are gall bladder stones and alcohol. Others include very high blood fats (triglycerides), high calcium, some medicines, injury to the abdomen and inherited factors. Finding the cause matters, because treating it prevents further attacks.

    13What are the warning signs?

    Severe pain in the upper belly that often spreads to the back, gets worse after eating and comes with vomiting needs hospital care the same day. Repeated pain, weight loss, oily or foul-smelling stools, or new diabetes can point to chronic pancreatitis.

    14How can I lower my risk of pancreatitis?

    Get gall bladder stones treated if they have caused pain or an attack. Avoid alcohol, do not smoke, keep a healthy weight, and keep triglycerides and diabetes under control. After one attack from gallstones, the gall bladder is usually removed to stop it happening again.

    15What should I eat after pancreatitis?

    Once recovered, eat small, regular meals that are low in fat and fried food, and drink enough water. Stop alcohol completely. People with chronic pancreatitis may need enzyme capsules with meals and checks for diabetes. Follow the diet plan your doctor gives you.

    16Which digestive cancers should I know about, and who is at risk?

    Cancers of the food pipe, stomach, liver, gall bladder, pancreas, colon and rectum. Risk is higher with tobacco in any form (smoking, gutkha, khaini), alcohol, obesity, long-standing gallstones, hepatitis B or C, cirrhosis, a family history of cancer, and age over 50. Gall bladder cancer is more common in eastern and northern India than in most of the world.

    17What are the early warning signs of digestive cancer?

    See a doctor if any of these lasts more than two to three weeks: difficulty swallowing, constant indigestion or vomiting, loss of appetite, unexplained weight loss, blood in the stool or black stools, a change in bowel habit, yellow eyes or skin, tiredness from low blood, or a lump in the belly. Most of these have harmless causes, but early checks catch cancer when it is most treatable.

    18Can digestive cancer be prevented?

    Many cases can be. Avoid all tobacco, limit or stop alcohol, take the hepatitis B vaccine, get tested and treated for hepatitis C, stay active and keep a healthy weight, and eat plenty of vegetables, fruit, whole grains and fibre with less processed and red meat. Get symptomatic gallstones treated as advised.

    19Should I have screening tests?

    Ask your doctor about bowel cancer screening (a stool test or colonoscopy) from about age 45 to 50, or earlier if a close relative had bowel cancer. People with cirrhosis or long-term hepatitis B usually need a liver ultrasound every six months. Anyone with warning signs should have tests such as endoscopy without waiting.

    20Is pancreatitis linked to pancreatic cancer?

    Long-standing chronic pancreatitis raises the risk of pancreatic cancer over many years, so regular follow-up matters. New diabetes after the age of 50, especially with weight loss or back pain, can sometimes be an early sign of pancreatic cancer and should be checked.

    Diseases & treatment
    
    
    

    

    Esophagus (food pipe)
    

    The food pipe carries food from the throat to the stomach. Trouble swallowing, burning in the chest or food coming back up are the usual signs that something is wrong.
    

    

    01GERD (acid reflux)

    What it is
    Acid from the stomach flows back up into the food pipe because the valve at its lower end is weak.
    Signs
    Heartburn, sour water in the mouth, chest burning after meals or when lying down, long-standing cough or a hoarse voice.
    How it is treated
    Most people improve with smaller meals, no food for 3 hours before bed, weight loss, raising the head of the bed, stopping smoking and alcohol, and acid-reducing medicines. Endoscopy is advised if it lasts long or there are warning signs. If medicines do not control it, a laparoscopic (keyhole) fundoplication rebuilds the valve.
    

    

    02Hiatal hernia

    What it is
    Part of the stomach slides up through the opening in the diaphragm into the chest.
    Signs
    Often none. It can cause reflux, chest fullness, difficulty swallowing or breathlessness after meals. A large one can twist and needs urgent care.
    How it is treated
    Small hernias with reflux are treated like GERD. Large or troublesome hernias are repaired by keyhole surgery: the stomach is brought back down, the opening is narrowed and a fundoplication is added to stop reflux.
    

    

    03Esophagus cancer

    What it is
    A cancer of the lining of the food pipe. Tobacco, alcohol and long-standing reflux increase the risk.
    Signs
    Swallowing that gets harder, first for solids and then for liquids, weight loss, pain behind the breastbone, vomiting, or a hoarse voice.
    How it is treated
    Endoscopy with biopsy confirms it and CT or PET scans show its spread. Treatment is usually chemotherapy with or without radiation, followed by esophagectomy (removal of the food pipe, which is replaced with the stomach), done open or by minimally invasive surgery. When cure is not possible, a stent helps the patient swallow again.
    

    

    04Achalasia cardia

    What it is
    The valve at the lower end of the food pipe does not relax and the food pipe stops pushing food down, so food gets stuck.
    Signs
    Difficulty swallowing both solids and liquids, bringing back undigested food, chest pain, cough at night and weight loss.
    How it is treated
    Barium swallow, endoscopy and manometry confirm it. The most lasting treatment is laparoscopic Heller myotomy, which cuts the tight muscle, with a partial fundoplication to prevent reflux. Balloon dilatation or POEM are options for some patients.
    

    

    05Corrosive stricture

    What it is
    Narrowing of the food pipe after swallowing acid or alkali, such as toilet cleaner, by accident or on purpose.
    Signs
    Swallowing becomes difficult a few weeks after the injury, with drooling, vomiting and weight loss. After swallowing a corrosive, do not make the person vomit; go to hospital at once.
    How it is treated
    Nutrition is kept up, sometimes with a feeding tube. Short narrowings are stretched with repeated endoscopic dilatation. Long or tight strictures need surgery that replaces the food pipe with the stomach or colon.
    

    

    

    

    

    Stomach
    

    The stomach stores and digests food. Long-lasting pain, vomiting, early fullness or black stools should always be checked.
    

    

    01Gastric ulcer

    What it is
    A sore in the lining of the stomach, most often caused by H. pylori infection or painkillers (NSAIDs), and made worse by smoking and alcohol.
    Signs
    Burning pain in the upper belly, often linked to meals, nausea and bloating. Danger signs: vomiting blood, black stools, or sudden severe pain.
    How it is treated
    Endoscopy confirms it and a biopsy rules out cancer. Acid-reducing medicines and antibiotics for H. pylori heal most ulcers. Surgery is needed for a perforation (a hole), bleeding that endoscopy cannot stop, or a blocked stomach outlet.
    

    

    02Stomach cancer

    What it is
    A cancer that starts in the stomach lining.
    Signs
    Indigestion that does not go away, feeling full early, loss of appetite, weight loss, vomiting, low blood count, or black stools.
    How it is treated
    Endoscopy with biopsy and a CT scan plan the treatment. Surgery removes part or all of the stomach with the nearby lymph nodes (gastrectomy), by laparoscopic or open surgery. Chemotherapy is often given before and after the operation.
    

    

    03Gastritis

    What it is
    Inflammation of the stomach lining, often due to H. pylori, painkillers, alcohol, irregular meals or stress.
    Signs
    Burning or dull upper belly pain, bloating, nausea and loss of appetite.
    How it is treated
    Medicines to reduce acid, treatment of H. pylori, and avoiding triggers. Endoscopy is advised if symptoms persist, return, or come with warning signs, especially after age 45. It does not need surgery.
    

    

    04Gastric GIST

    What it is
    A gastrointestinal stromal tumour, which grows from the muscle wall of the stomach.
    Signs
    Often found by chance on a scan or endoscopy. It can bleed (black stools, weakness) or cause fullness or a lump.
    How it is treated
    Surgery removes the tumour with a margin of healthy tissue, often by laparoscopic wedge resection. Prof. Patnaik has published a laparoscopic technique for this. Imatinib tablets are added for large or high-risk tumours.
    

    

    

    

    

    Liver
    

    The liver filters blood, makes bile and stores energy. Many liver diseases stay silent for years, so tests matter when there is a risk.
    

    

    01Jaundice

    What it is
    Yellowing of the eyes and skin from bilirubin building up. It is a sign, not a disease. Causes include hepatitis, alcohol, stones or growths blocking the bile duct, and some blood disorders.
    Signs
    Yellow eyes, dark urine, pale stools, itching. Jaundice with fever and shivering, or with weight loss, needs to be seen quickly.
    How it is treated
    Blood tests and an ultrasound or MRCP find the cause. A blockage is relieved with an ERCP stent or by surgery; liver causes are treated with medicines. Herbal treatments can delay the diagnosis and are best avoided.
    

    

    02Hepatitis

    What it is
    Inflammation of the liver, most often from viruses. Hepatitis A and E spread through contaminated food and water; B and C through blood, unsafe needles, sex, or from mother to baby. Alcohol and fatty liver can also cause it.
    Signs
    Tiredness, fever, nausea, loss of appetite and jaundice. Hepatitis B and C are often silent for years.
    How it is treated
    Hepatitis A and E usually settle with rest and fluids. Hepatitis B is controlled and hepatitis C is cured with tablets. Vaccines protect against A and B. Long-term damage such as cirrhosis or liver cancer is where liver surgery and transplant come in.
    

    

    03Cirrhosis

    What it is
    Long-term scarring of the liver, usually from alcohol, hepatitis B or C, or fatty liver.
    Signs
    Tiredness, a swollen belly (fluid) and swollen legs, jaundice, vomiting blood, easy bruising, and confusion.
    How it is treated
    Treating the cause (stopping alcohol completely, antiviral medicines), a good diet, and medicines for fluid and bleeding. A liver ultrasound every 6 months checks for cancer. Advanced cirrhosis is treated with liver transplantation.
    

    

    04Fatty liver

    What it is
    Fat building up in the liver, linked with excess weight, diabetes, high cholesterol and alcohol.
    Signs
    Usually none. It is found on an ultrasound or blood test. Over years it can lead to inflammation, scarring and cirrhosis.
    How it is treated
    Losing 7 to 10% of body weight, daily exercise, less sugar and fried food, controlling diabetes and cholesterol, and avoiding alcohol. For severe obesity, bariatric (metabolic) surgery can reverse it.
    

    

    05Liver cancer

    What it is
    Cancer that starts in the liver, usually in a liver with cirrhosis or hepatitis B, or cancer that has spread to the liver from another organ such as the colon.
    Signs
    Often silent early. Later: pain in the upper right belly, weight loss, a lump, or jaundice.
    How it is treated
    CT or MRI and blood tests plan the treatment. Options are surgical removal of part of the liver (liver resection), liver transplant for selected patients, ablation, TACE, and targeted or immune therapy.
    

    

    06Hydatid cyst

    What it is
    A cyst formed by a tapeworm, usually caught from dogs or from contaminated food and water.
    Signs
    Often none. It can cause upper right belly pain or a lump. If it bursts it can cause a serious allergic reaction.
    How it is treated
    Albendazole tablets are given before and after treatment. The cyst is removed by surgery, often laparoscopic, taking care that its contents do not spill. Some cysts can be treated by needle drainage (PAIR).
    

    

    07Liver abscess

    What it is
    A collection of pus in the liver, either amoebic (from contaminated water) or bacterial.
    Signs
    High fever with chills, pain in the upper right belly or right shoulder, and loss of appetite.
    How it is treated
    Antibiotics or anti-amoebic medicines, and drainage with a needle or tube under ultrasound. Surgery is needed only if it bursts or does not respond.
    

    

    08Liver transplant

    What it is
    Replacing a failed liver with a healthy one, from a deceased donor or as part of the liver from a living family donor. Both the donor's liver and the transplanted part grow back to normal size.
    Signs
    Needed for end-stage cirrhosis with complications, sudden liver failure, and some liver cancers.
    How it is treated
    Careful assessment of the patient and donor, the transplant operation, and lifelong medicines to prevent rejection with regular follow-up. Prof. Patnaik performed Odisha's first liver transplant.
    

    

    

    

    

    Gall bladder & bile duct
    

    The gall bladder stores bile, and the bile ducts carry it from the liver to the intestine. Stones are the most common problem; jaundice is a sign of a blocked duct.
    

    

    01Gall bladder stone

    What it is
    Stones that form inside the gall bladder.
    Signs
    Pain in the upper right or middle belly, often after oily food, spreading to the back or right shoulder, with nausea or vomiting. Some stones cause no symptoms.
    How it is treated
    Stones that cause pain or complications are treated by laparoscopic cholecystectomy (keyhole removal of the gall bladder). Most patients go home in 1 to 2 days. Medicines do not reliably dissolve stones.
    

    

    02Bile duct stone

    What it is
    A stone that has slipped into, or formed in, the main bile duct.
    Signs
    Jaundice, upper belly pain, and fever with chills (an emergency called cholangitis). It can also cause pancreatitis.
    How it is treated
    The stone is removed by ERCP (through an endoscope), followed by laparoscopic removal of the gall bladder. Some patients have both done in one keyhole operation.
    

    

    03Gall bladder cancer

    What it is
    A cancer of the gall bladder, more common in eastern and northern India. Long-standing stones and polyps over 1 cm raise the risk.
    Signs
    Often silent early. Later: pain, weight loss, loss of appetite, a lump or jaundice.
    How it is treated
    When found early it is treated by radical cholecystectomy: removal of the gall bladder with part of the liver and the lymph nodes, by laparoscopic surgery in selected patients (Prof. Patnaik has published on this). Advanced disease is treated with chemotherapy and stents for jaundice. Treating troublesome gallstones in time lowers the risk.
    

    

    04Bile duct cancer

    What it is
    Cancer of the bile ducts (cholangiocarcinoma).
    Signs
    Painless jaundice, itching, pale stools, dark urine and weight loss.
    How it is treated
    MRI and CT show where it is. Surgery removes the affected duct, with part of the liver or a Whipple operation depending on its position. A stent relieves jaundice, and chemotherapy is used before or after surgery or when surgery is not possible.
    

    

    05Bile duct stricture

    What it is
    Narrowing of the bile duct after an injury during gall bladder surgery, or from stones, pancreatitis, or a tumour.
    Signs
    Jaundice, itching, and fever with chills.
    How it is treated
    An endoscopic stent or balloon dilatation relieves the blockage. The lasting repair is surgery joining the bile duct to the intestine (hepaticojejunostomy).
    

    

    

    

    

    Pancreas
    

    The pancreas makes digestive juices and insulin. Severe upper belly pain going to the back, new diabetes with weight loss, or painless jaundice need prompt checks.
    

    

    01Pancreatitis

    What it is
    Inflammation of the pancreas. Acute pancreatitis comes suddenly, most often from gallstones or alcohol. Chronic pancreatitis is long-lasting damage.
    Signs
    Severe upper belly pain going to the back, with vomiting (acute). Repeated pain, weight loss, oily stools and diabetes (chronic).
    How it is treated
    Acute: hospital care with fluids, pain relief and nutrition, then removing the cause, for example taking out the gall bladder. Severe cases may need drainage or removal of infected dead tissue (necrosectomy), an area Prof. Patnaik researched at AIIMS. Chronic: pain control, enzyme capsules, diabetes care, and endoscopic or surgical drainage of the duct for lasting pain.
    

    

    02Pancreas cancer

    What it is
    Cancer of the pancreas.
    Signs
    Painless jaundice, upper belly or back pain, weight loss, loss of appetite, pale stools, or new diabetes after age 50.
    How it is treated
    CT scan and sometimes endoscopic ultrasound plan the treatment. Tumours in the head of the pancreas are removed by the Whipple operation (pancreaticoduodenectomy), those in the body or tail by distal pancreatectomy. Chemotherapy is given before or after surgery. Stents or bypass relieve jaundice and blockage when surgery is not possible.
    

    

    03Pancreas pseudocyst

    What it is
    A collection of fluid that forms after pancreatitis.
    Signs
    Fullness, pain, vomiting, or a lump in the upper belly. Many cause no symptoms.
    How it is treated
    Many go away on their own over weeks. Large, painful or long-lasting ones are drained into the stomach or intestine, by endoscopy or by laparoscopic or open surgery.
    

    

    04Cystic neoplasm of pancreas

    What it is
    Fluid-filled growths in the pancreas (such as IPMN, mucinous or serous cysts), often found by chance on a scan. Some are harmless; others can turn into cancer.
    Signs
    Usually none. Larger ones can cause pain, pancreatitis or jaundice.
    How it is treated
    MRI and endoscopic ultrasound show which type it is. Small, low-risk cysts are watched with regular scans. Cysts with high-risk features are removed surgically.
    

    

    

    

    

    Colon, rectum & anus
    

    The large bowel absorbs water and forms stool. Any bleeding from the back passage, or a change in bowel habit lasting more than a few weeks, should be examined.
    

    

    01Colon cancer

    What it is
    Cancer of the large bowel, usually starting from a polyp.
    Signs
    A change in bowel habit, blood in the stool, low blood count, tiredness, belly pain, weight loss or a lump. Screening from about age 45 to 50 finds it early.
    How it is treated
    Colonoscopy with biopsy and a CT scan plan the treatment. Surgery removes the affected part of the colon with its lymph nodes, usually by laparoscopic surgery. Chemotherapy is added for more advanced stages.
    

    

    02Rectal cancer

    What it is
    Cancer of the last part of the large bowel.
    Signs
    Bleeding from the back passage, mucus, a feeling of not emptying fully, or a change in bowel habit. Bleeding should not be assumed to be piles without a check.
    How it is treated
    MRI shows the stage. Treatment often begins with chemotherapy and radiation, followed by surgery that removes the rectum while saving the anal sphincter wherever possible, often laparoscopically. A temporary stoma is sometimes needed while the join heals.
    

    

    03Ulcerative colitis

    What it is
    Long-term inflammation of the inner lining of the colon and rectum.
    Signs
    Diarrhoea with blood and mucus, urgency, cramps, tiredness and weight loss, coming in flare-ups.
    How it is treated
    Medicines (mesalamine, steroids, immunomodulators, biologics) control most cases. Surgery that removes the colon, with an internal pouch so the patient can pass stool normally, is used for severe attacks that do not respond, complications, or cancer risk.
    

    

    04Crohn's disease

    What it is
    Inflammation that can affect any part of the gut, often the end of the small intestine, through the full thickness of the wall. In India it can look like intestinal tuberculosis and needs careful testing.
    Signs
    Belly pain, diarrhoea, weight loss, fistulas or abscesses around the anus, and bowel blockage.
    How it is treated
    Medicines control the inflammation. Surgery is used for narrowing, blockage, abscess or fistula, and removes only the diseased segment to save as much bowel as possible.
    

    

    05Colon polyp

    What it is
    A growth on the lining of the colon. Some types (adenomas) can slowly turn into cancer.
    Signs
    Usually none. Sometimes bleeding or low blood count.
    How it is treated
    Polyps are removed during colonoscopy (polypectomy). Very large polyps or those containing cancer need surgery. Follow-up colonoscopy checks for new ones.
    

    

    06Piles

    What it is
    Swollen blood vessels in and around the anus (haemorrhoids).
    Signs
    Painless bright red bleeding with stools, a lump that comes out, itching or discomfort.
    How it is treated
    Fibre, plenty of water, not straining, warm sitz baths and medicines help most people. Banding or injection treats moderate piles. Large piles are treated by surgery (stapler, laser or conventional haemorrhoidectomy). Bleeding after age 40 should be checked to rule out cancer.
    

    

    07Anal fissure

    What it is
    A small tear in the lining of the anus, usually from hard stools.
    Signs
    Sharp pain during and after passing stool, lasting minutes to hours, with a few drops of blood.
    How it is treated
    Most heal in 4 to 8 weeks with fibre, water, stool softeners, sitz baths and ointments that relax the muscle. A long-standing fissure is cured by a minor operation (lateral internal sphincterotomy).
    

    

    08Fistula

    What it is
    A fistula-in-ano: a small tunnel between the inside of the anus and the skin near it, usually after an abscess.
    Signs
    Repeated pus or discharge near the anus, pain, swelling and soiling of clothes.
    How it is treated
    A fistula does not heal with medicines and needs surgery. The type is mapped first (sometimes with MRI) and treated by fistulotomy, seton, LIFT or laser, taking care to protect bowel control.
    

    

    

    

    

    Abdominal wall
    

    A hernia is a weak spot in the muscles of the belly wall that lets tissue bulge through.
    

    

    01Hernia

    What it is
    A bulge in the groin, at the navel or at an old operation scar, where tissue pushes through a weak spot in the abdominal wall.
    Signs
    A swelling that appears on standing or coughing and goes back on lying down, with an ache. A hernia that becomes painful and will not go back is an emergency.
    How it is treated
    Hernias do not heal on their own. They are repaired with a mesh, usually by laparoscopic surgery (TEP or TAPP) or by open surgery, often with a short hospital stay.
